Questions we'll have to answer to scale

Things the model surfaced but deliberately doesn't answer. Each is parked here with what we already know and what would settle it — so we're not rediscovering the same tension in three months.

01open

Should onboarding and long-term account maintenance eventually be separate people?

You can only run so many onboardings a month, but recurring accounts stack indefinitely. Every client added keeps drawing maintenance hours forever, so the maintenance load grows while onboarding capacity stays flat. At some point one person cannot hold both.

The tension: the dev who built a client's system maintains it far more cheaply than anyone else, because the context is in their head. Handing it over means paying someone to slowly learn what Geri already knows. But context in a head is a choice, not a law. The same factory work that takes onboarding from 100 hours to 40 — specs, reusable adapters, runbooks, monitoring — is exactly what makes maintenance transferable. One investment, not two.

What we already know
  • At 96 of Geri's delivery hours a month and 12 hrs upkeep per client, maintenance alone consumes him entirely at 8 live clients.
  • The model puts the two-person ceiling at 9–14 live clients — so the maintenance wall and the capacity ceiling arrive at the same moment. Not a distant problem.
  • Halving upkeep to 6 hrs moves the wall to 16 clients — the same lever that fixes everything else in this model.
  • A likely answer is splitting by problem type, not by person: recurring failures, monitoring and data hygiene delegate cleanly; novel breakage and change requests don't.
What would settle it

Track, from client #1, what share of maintenance is recurring versus novel. If most fixes are the same handful of failure modes across clients, maintenance is delegable and the hire is cheap. If every client breaks differently, it stays welded to the builder — and that argues for tighter qualification, not a bigger team.

02verify

How many hours does a live client actually consume each month?

The most load-bearing assumption in the model, and currently a guess. It sets the capacity ceiling, the parity price for the recurring tier, and when the maintenance wall arrives.

What we already know
  • Currently assumed 12 hrs/month per live client, scaling down with efficiency.
  • At 12 hrs, recurring parity sits at $2,400/mo — so $3,000 is only 1.25× ahead.
  • At 6 hrs, parity drops to $1,200 and $3,000 becomes 2.5× ahead. Halving upkeep does the same work as doubling the price, without a pricing conversation.
What would settle it

Geri logs maintenance hours per client for the first two clients. Two months of real data replaces the most consequential guess in the model.

03verify

What does it actually take to make the apps usable by outside companies — and when do we pay for it?

The machines were built single-tenant, running on our own data. Client #1 forces the question: separate logins per client, isolation so one client can never see another's pipeline, per-client secrets, and config that isn't hardcoded to QS. In plain terms, it's the difference between a house you built for yourself and an apartment building — same structure, but it now needs its own front doors and walls that actually work.

It was briefly in the model at 80 hours, then taken out, and taking it out was right: it is paid once and never again, so it isn't a per-client cost and doesn't belong among levers that are all variable. But it doesn't disappear — it's a prerequisite that lands before any revenue does.

What we already know
  • The 80 hr figure is a guess, and the least-informed number in anything we've estimated.
  • If the apps were written with tenancy in mind it could be 30 hrs. If QS is hardcoded through the schema it could be 150.
  • At 160 delivery hours a month, 80 hrs is half of your first month spent before client #1 produces anything.
What would settle it

Geri reviews the current apps against one question: what breaks if a second company's data lands in this? An afternoon of his time replaces the guess, and it's the one estimate that changes when you can start.

04open

Should efficiency improve with clients delivered rather than with time?

The model currently improves efficiency on a monthly timer. But you learn by shipping, not by waiting — a month with two onboardings should teach more than a month with none. Driving the curve off completed clients would make early throughput self-reinforcing, and would correctly punish a slow start.

What would settle it

Straightforward to change once there's real data on how much cheaper client #3 was than client #1.

05open

Do we refuse clients on non-standard stacks?

A client on an exotic CRM could cost 2–3× a normal onboarding and eat a month. Worse, their maintenance never standardises — so they stay welded to Geri permanently rather than ever becoming delegable. A stronger reason to decline than the onboarding hours alone.

What would settle it

Probably a qualification rule in the audit — we deploy onto these systems — rather than a lever in the model.

06parked

The model is deliberately pessimistic about retention. How much?

Expansion revenue is switched off, referrals aren't modelled, and a retained client's biggest hidden benefit — that it needs no sales or pre-sales time at all — is invisible here. Every one of those pushes the same way, so any conclusion this model reaches about keeping clients is a floor, not a forecast.

07parked

The hiring extension — what happens past the two-person ceiling?

Phase 2 of the model. Delivery hires first, then when a commission-only closer becomes viable — they need 5 deals a month to clear $10k at 10% of upfront, well beyond what two people deliver.

08locked

Menu only. We do not do bespoke, and we say so out front.

Ten builds, each configured per client the way an Oracle or SAP implementation is configured — but no genuinely bespoke work, ever. Declaring it publicly converts a per-deal negotiation into a positioning decision: the client self-selects before you speak, and off-menu would require contradicting your own site.

Upsells are constrained to the same menu. The mechanism that makes this hold is not discipline — it is that architects have no price to quote off-menu. Remove the ability rather than relying on willpower.

Why now is the moment

You are delivery-bound today, so declining an off-menu client costs nothing — you were going to be full anyway. At the far end of the arc you are lead-bound, and every declined deal is a permanent loss. The habit is free to build now and expensive to start later.

09locked

What makes a request menu-able — and how we decide without taste

Four tests. It needs all four:

1. High perceived value — it visibly moves revenue, profit or margin, so they assign real money to it. 2. Repeatable — not so unique per client that every version is a fresh build. 3. Creates ongoing dependency — it runs continuously on our infrastructure rather than handing them an artifact they then own. Retention is worth roughly 3× what pricing is worth, so two requests can score identically on value and repeatability and be worth wildly different amounts to us. 4. Delegable — someone who is not Lloyd or Geri can build it.

The 2×2 that matters: high-value-but-bespoke is the dangerous cell, because the money is real and the temptation is maximum. Low-value-but-repeatable is the quiet one — it produces menu bloat, and ten things you are famous for beat twenty-five things you offer.

How this survives you not being in the room

Taste does not scale. So the architect’s job at the point of sale is to log it and decline it, never to evaluate it. Evaluation happens later, in batch, by whoever owns the menu.

Then replace prediction with a counter. “Other people will want this” is a forecast. Build it when 3–5 distinct clients have asked for substantially the same thing. Waiting costs nothing — you were declining it either way — and the log becomes a product roadmap that writes itself.

10open

Are we loved, or merely depended upon?

The recurring fee is not maintenance labour — it is continued operation of infrastructure the client has restructured around, running on our servers. Cancelling does not stop a service, it removes a capability. That is a genuinely strong retention mechanism and it deepens as customisation accumulates.

But lock-in and loyalty look identical on a churn report. Clients who stay because leaving is hard churn at the first opening — a budget cut, a new CTO, a competitor offering free migration — and critically, they never refer. That caps growth precisely where the model already says you are capped: lead supply.

What would settle it

At month six, ask your first clients what they would do if a competitor offered to rebuild it and migrate them free. Their hesitation is the real number. Retention is worth ~3× pricing in this model, so this is the single most valuable question you can ask a client.

11open

Hosting it means we now have to run it like it matters — and that is not costed

The consequence of the answer above. You cannot claim “mission-critical, lives on our servers” without accepting the obligation that comes with it:

• Upkeep at 12 hrs/client/month is a maintenance figure, not an operations figure. Monitoring, incident response and API changes breaking things overnight are a different cost.
• An outage becomes a mass event. Shared infrastructure means one bad deploy hits everyone at once — the failure mode is a churn wave, not one unhappy client.
• You are carrying liability. Automation touching client CRM data and sending on their behalf means contracts, insurance and a security posture across the whole book.

Why it matters at exit, not just operationally

An acquirer looking at critical infrastructure for hundreds of clients with no SOC 2, no redundancy and no runbooks applies a discount at diligence. Cheap to build the discipline early; expensive to retrofit at the LOI stage.

12open

How many clients are we willing to hold at launch pricing, permanently?

Pricing low to make it easy to join is a reasonable launch decision, and it has a hidden benefit — a low recurring fee means the “why am I paying this?” question never gets asked, so underpricing is functionally a retention strategy as well as an acquisition one.

But price is grandfathered at signing in this model, which is correct and also means every client signed cheap is cheap forever. The clients acquired in the next twelve months are the ones still being served in 2029 at 2026 prices.

The decision to make deliberately

Not “should I price low to start” but “how many clients am I willing to hold at this price before raising it?” Pick the number now rather than drifting into it.

13open

Will a hired architect really close at 90% of Lloyd’s rate?

The last failure mode still standing. The audit is not a pitch — it is pattern recognition across dozens of businesses, which is exactly the thing that takes years to acquire. The model assumes a hire does it at 90% of a CRO’s rate.

The sensitivity is not subtle: at 50% of your rate the arc terminates around $5.7m/yr; at 90% it reaches $8.6m/yr. A miss here does not slow you down, it caps you — because this hire is the only thing that lifts the ceiling once your own calendar binds.

What would settle it

Nothing but watching someone try. It cannot be reasoned away, and it is the strongest argument for hiring the first architect earlier than the model triggers it, purely to learn the answer while it is still cheap to be wrong.

14open

Second channel: when, and which one?

Invites are the terminal constraint but not a near-term one. They first bind at M27 — delivery and your own calendar bind for the 26 months before that. More leads before then convert to nothing.

Paid is a multiplier on unit economics, not a fix for them, so it is downstream of proving retention. Your LTV headroom is a genuine advantage against bootstrapped competitors and roughly worthless against venture-funded ones in the same auction — you cannot out-lose someone burning venture money by design. Small spend early for learning, scale when delivery can absorb it.

AEO layers on top of SEO — LLMs read page one of an index, so you cannot be cited if you do not rank. SEO-shaped timelines. Baseline measurement at M0–M3 (cheap, and worthless if taken after content exists), build at M9–M15 so it lands before M27.

The decision rule

Build the cheap mid-funnel version — a few extremely credible pages plus third-party mentions. At matched ambition cold discovery is worth more ($14.8m vs $11.5m at +33% on each lever), and mid-funnel would need the close rate near 45% to match it — but the two converge at modest ambition, and the cheap build is probably sufficient. If the ceiling ever needs lifting by more than ~20%, the cheap version cannot get there and the expensive one is live again.

Dependency: the credible case-study pages are substantially the same assets the marketing site is already blocked on. Demo videos are now on the critical path for two things.

15verify

The model’s “net” is not EBITDA — do not value the business off it

Payroll here counts hire salaries only: delivery, managers, architects, client success. It excludes founder compensation, ad spend, tooling and infrastructure, admin, legal and insurance — and it charges nothing for Lloyd’s or Geri’s time.

So the net line is contribution after hire payroll, not profit. A realistic EBITDA for a well-run productized service is roughly 45–55% of revenue, well below what this model shows.

Why it matters

Every exit-value conversation runs off EBITDA. Using the model’s net overstates the business by roughly a third and would put a $100m label on something closer to $60m.

16locked

Pricing: flat per menu item, with an annual uplift clause

Borrowed from MSPs, who charge per seat or per device so revenue grows automatically as the client grows, with no sales conversation. A flat monthly fee does not do that — a client who doubles their sales team pays you exactly the same as the day they signed.

But raw usage metering would be a mistake here, for three reasons:

• Unpredictable bills breed resentment. If every menu item meters separately and every meter moves monthly, nobody can budget and every invoice is a small argument.
• Never price on a unit the client cannot see. API calls, tokens and compute are invisible to them — they have no idea how many calls a workflow makes, so the bill feels arbitrary and unauditable.
• Worst of all, it inverts the incentive. If usage costs money, the rational client uses it less — you would be charging them for succeeding with the thing you sold them.

The decision — flat per menu item, plus an annual uplift clause

Each menu item has one flat monthly price. Transparent, explainable in a sentence, holdable in your head on a sales call. No metering, no fluctuating invoice, no argument every month.

Plus a contractual annual price adjustment, capped at a stated percentage — 5–7%, or CPI-linked. Ubiquitous in enterprise SaaS and MSP contracts, and a buyer of the business will expect to see it.

Three reasons it is lower risk than not having it: the optionality is the value whether or not you exercise it; it protects margin against your own rising salaries; and it must exist from day one — price is grandfathered at signing, so without this clause every cheap client is cheap permanently and the only way to reprice is to reopen the whole contract.

The rule that makes it safe: use it every year at a small number, never dormant then large. A 5% annual uplift is background noise. A clause that sleeps for three years and then fires at 25% reads as opportunistic and makes the client re-examine the entire relationship at exactly the wrong moment.

Mechanics: adjustment on the anniversary, stated cap, 30–60 days written notice. A “no increase in year one” grace makes it easier to sell and costs almost nothing.

When they push back on the clause

Do not cap it lower. Trade price certainty for contract length: “We will freeze your price for three years if you commit for three.” They get the budget certainty they actually want; you get a multi-year contract, which is a direct multiple driver on the Exit scorecard. A pricing concession converted into a valuation asset.

Be clear-eyed about what this is. With the uplift as the only mechanism on existing clients, net revenue retention caps around 105%. That is margin protection, not a growth engine. Real expansion comes from installing more menu items. Do not let the clause make expansion feel solved.

Parked variant: size-banded pricing — the flat price sits in a band set by one client-size metric they already track, reviewed annually. Captures client growth automatically rather than just inflation. Additive later and non-conflicting: since price is fixed at signing, it can be introduced for new cohorts without touching a single existing client.

What would settle it

Pick the size metric before the first contract, because it is nearly impossible to change later — it has to be something every client in the ICP has, tracks accurately, and cannot game. Ask in the first ten audits what number they would consider fair to be priced against. Their answer is the metric.

What this person actually did before

Post-sales at a B2B SaaS company splits into three separate functions. They are not the same job and they are not the same people:

FunctionOwnsShape
Professional Services / ImplementationGetting the customer live — fast, cheap, repeatablyBounded. Has a start and an end. A production problem.

You want the first one. Specifically a Director or Head of Professional Services / Implementation. Not a CS leader — that is a commercial, relationship-management skill set and it is a different hire, which the model already accounts for separately.

Their week, concretely

  • Runs a team of implementation consultants, each carrying 8–15 customer onboardings at once, all at different stages
  • Weekly portfolio review: where every account sits, what is blocked, who is overloaded
  • Owns the playbook — stages, templates, checklists, standard integrations, the QA gate before go-live
  • Takes escalations when an implementation goes sideways
  • Pushes recurring friction back into the product — “every customer hits this, stop making us solve it by hand”
  • Hires and trains consultants; owns how fast a new one becomes productive
  • Reports weekly on time-to-live, cost-to-implement, services margin, and % of implementations on time

That fifth point is the one that matters most for you. Pushing friction back into the product is standard behaviour in this role — it is exactly the loop you described where you invent, Geri makes it work once, and then it has to be made deliverable 50 times. A PS leader has done that loop for a living. A dev-shop ops leader has usually never had a product to push anything back into.

Do they own retention and churn?

They own the leading indicator, not the number. PS leaders are measured on time-to-value and onboarding quality precisely because those are the biggest single predictor of whether a customer renews. But they do not run the renewal conversation, the QBR, or the upsell. That is Client Success.

So: a good Head of Delivery reduces churn structurally, by making sure every client gets to value fast and consistently. They do not reduce it by managing relationships. If you find yourself asking them to save an account, something has gone wrong upstream.

Who owns adoption?

It is split, and the split point is go-live.

  • Head of Delivery owns adoption up to and through go-live — the handover, the training, the “your team can actually operate this without us” gate.
  • Client Success owns adoption after go-live — ongoing usage, drift, the champion leaving, expansion.

This is why serious implementation teams measure time-to-value, not time-to-live. Working software that nobody uses is not a delivered implementation, it is a delivered invoice.

The handoff is where churn is manufactured. The classic failure: delivery declares victory at “it works”, client success inherits an account that never actually adopted, and nobody notices until four months later when the renewal conversation lands and usage is flat. By then it is unrecoverable, and it will look like a client success failure when it was a handoff failure.

The fix, and it is a one-line policy: an implementation is not complete at go-live. It is complete when a defined adoption threshold is met — something measurable, like the client has run this themselves for three consecutive weeks without our involvement. Until then it stays on the delivery team’s board and keeps consuming a slot.

That single rule does three things: it stops delivery gaming the metric by declaring done early, it makes the handoff an event rather than a drift, and — in this model — it correctly charges the cost of a slow-adopting client to delivery capacity, where it actually falls.

Your version is blurrier than standard SaaS. Your delivery org never fully leaves — every live client carries permanent monthly upkeep. So the delivery team stays in contact indefinitely, which pushes more of the adoption burden onto them than a classic implementation team would carry. Worth being explicit about in the job description, because a candidate from pure SaaS PS will assume they hand off and walk away.

And the reason this matters more for you than for them: at a SaaS company the product still exists whether the customer uses it or not. In your business, the client’s team using the workflow is the entire deliverable. No adoption means there was never anything to retain. Adoption is not a nice-to-have layered on top of delivery here — it is delivery.

We deliberately do not model adoption as a number. It is too situational to put a slider on, and a false precision here would be worse than an honest gap. It is handled where it actually lives — in who owns it, and in the handoff below.

How the Head of Delivery thinks about adoption

The distinguishing belief, and it is worth testing for directly in the interview: adoption is a delivery outcome, not a customer-success favour.

A weak candidate treats adoption as somebody else’s problem that starts after their work ends. A strong one designs for it during the build, because they know the two are the same thing:

  • At design time they ask who on the client’s side will operate this, and what is their actual skill level? A workflow that only the client’s most technical person can run is a workflow that dies when that person is busy.
  • During the build they decide what the client operates versus what stays with you. Getting that line wrong in either direction is fatal — too much on them and it goes unused, too much on you and your upkeep hours never fall.
  • At handover they own the training. Not a recorded walkthrough — the client’s people doing the thing, with the delivery person watching and correcting.
  • For the first few weeks after go-live they stay close, watching whether it is actually being run, and fixing friction fast while attention is still high.

The tell in an interview: ask what “done” meant on their last team. If the answer is a technical milestone — live, deployed, in production — they are a delivery manager. If it is a usage condition, they are what you need.

  • Known fragilities — what will break first, and what to do when it does
  • Anything promised in the sale that has not yet been delivered

Held as a live meeting with the client’s champion present, not a document thrown over a wall. The champion needs to meet the person who now owns them, or client success starts from zero relationship on an account they did not build.

What delivery keeps after handoff: the monthly upkeep hours and technical escalation. They do not vanish — your model has them attached to every live client permanently. What changes is that they are no longer driving; they are responding.

Four ways this handoff fails, all of them common:

• No defined trigger — the handoff never formally happens, both sides assume the other has it, and the account is orphaned in plain sight.
• Handed off on a date — “90 days post-signing” hands over non-adopted clients on schedule, which is worse than not handing off at all.
• No artifact — client success starts blind, asks the client questions the client already answered, and burns the credibility delivery built.
• Delivery disappears completely — technical questions bounce back and forth, the client concludes nobody owns them, and you have manufactured a churn risk out of pure process.

One wrinkle specific to your model. Your delivery org does not only onboard — it also carries permanent monthly upkeep on every live client. That is more managed-services shaped than pure SaaS implementation. The ideal candidate is therefore PS-primary with some managed-services or post-launch-ownership exposure, not someone who has only ever handed off at go-live and walked away.

Why SaaS beats a web dev shop

It is not about skills. It is about which direction their incentive has always pointed.

A dev shop makes money from utilisation. Revenue is hours × rate. The ops leader keeps people billable and stops fixed bids blowing up. Repeatability is actively against their interest — a dev shop that productises loses the ability to charge custom prices. The model needs every job to be different enough to justify a bespoke quote.

A SaaS implementation team is a cost centre attached to recurring revenue. Their mandate every quarter is: make onboarding cheaper and faster, because it is dragging gross margin and delaying time-to-value. They are measured on cost-to-serve going down. They are structurally paid to eliminate their own work.

Your model only works if cost per client falls. Hire the person whose entire career has been that one metric.

  • Retention orientation. SaaS PS people connect onboarding quality to churn by reflex. A dev shop delivers and moves on — the project ending is success. Your model has retention as one of its largest levers.
  • The deliverable is bounded. In SaaS PS the product exists; implementation is configuration, integration, training and change management inside known bounds. That is your shape exactly. Dev shops invent the deliverable each time.
  • Parallelism at the individual level. A SaaS implementation consultant carries 8–15 concurrent accounts as a matter of routine. Dev shop ICs carry one or two, deep — and their ops leaders protect them from parallelism, because context-switching wrecks deep engineering. Opposite instinct to the one you need.
  • Stage-gated pipelines. They run onboarding as a board with stages and work-in-progress limits, and manage the portfolio rather than the project. That is literally the mechanic in this model.

The decoys — what will trick you

Impressive logos

A Director of PS at Salesforce inherited a machine 200 people built over 15 years. They can run a machine. They have never built one. The person from a company you have never heard of who built it from zero is worth more and costs less.

“I managed 40” beats “I managed 6” in your gut, and is wrong. Managing 40 in a mature org is a different job. You want 5–15 in a scrappy one.

Domain expertise in AI or agencies

Feels safe. Domain is learnable in a quarter; industrialisation takes a decade. Never trade the rare skill for the common one.

They will meet clients occasionally. The job is roughly 85% internal. Do not select for whoever interviews best.

Everyone says this. What matters is whether developers respected them. Take a developer reference specifically and ask that person one question: did you follow their process when nobody was checking?

Feels perfect — they ran a services business. But they are usually generalists who did everything, and they want to run a company, not a function. High risk of role conflict with you.

This is the one that will actually get you. It sounds like exactly the right attitude for a small company. How to test them

“Walk me through a delivery process you took from bespoke to repeatable. What did it cost before, what after, and how long did it take?”

The core screen. Project people tell a story. Industrialisers give you two numbers.

“Tell me about a time you told a founder their idea could not be delivered at scale — and what happened.”

Weight this highest. If they have never done it they will be a passenger to your innovation, and you will end up with designs that cannot be manufactured.

“What is the first thing you do here?”

“Run a few onboardings myself to learn the work” is right. “Write a strategy document” is weak. “Hire people” is disqualifying.

Give them a real recording and notes from one of your onboardings and ask them to spec how they would make it repeatable. Half a day, a few hundred dollars. It will tell you more than every interview combined.

What this role is not

Not a classic SaaS customer success manager running quarterly business reviews and a renewal calendar. That version of the job assumes a product the customer logs into daily and a renewal date twelve months out. Neither is true here.

Your clients are founders of $300k–$10M businesses. They did not buy software. They bought a system that is supposed to run without them thinking about it — which means the failure mode is silent. Nothing errors. Nobody complains. The workflow just quietly stops being used, and six weeks later they cannot tell you what they are paying for.

The whole job is catching silence early. An unhappy client tells you. A disengaged one does not — they simply cancel, and you find out on the invoice.

What they own

  • Usage stays alive. Someone is running the workflows, and it is still producing the outcome that was sold.
  • The champion risk. The person who bought and drove this internally leaves, changes role, or gets busy. This is the single most common cause of quiet churn in any implemented system, and it is entirely detectable in advance.
  • Proving the value out loud. Founders forget. Somebody has to periodically show them the number the system produced, in their language, or the fee starts feeling arbitrary.
  • The next build. Expansion is not a separate sales motion here — it is noticing that a client who automated one thing has an obvious second thing, and routing that back to you or an architect.
  • The early-warning signal. They are the ones who tell delivery that a build type keeps decaying after month four, which is a delivery problem wearing a retention costume.

Adoption and internal buy-in — the core of the job

This is the part that is easy to underweight and is actually the whole role. What you sell changes how somebody inside that business does their work. That is not a technical event, it is a people event, and it can fail completely while every system runs perfectly.

The founder bought it. The team has to live with it. Those are different people with different incentives, and the second group was usually not in the room. A founder who is excited and a team who were told on Monday is the single most common way a working build produces zero value.

What that actually looks like in practice:

  • Someone inside must own it. Not the founder — they are too busy and it will drift. A named person on the client’s team whose job now visibly includes this. If nobody owns it, it belongs to nobody, and it decays from week one.
  • A visible early win, fast. The team needs one concrete moment where this obviously saved them something — a lead handled overnight, a report that used to take an afternoon. Abstract efficiency arguments do not change behaviour; one visible win does.
  • The old way has to get harder or disappear. If the spreadsheet still exists, people use the spreadsheet. Adoption is as much about retiring the old process as introducing the new one, and that is a conversation with the founder, not a technical task.
  • Watch for the threat reaction. Some of the client’s staff will read automation as aimed at their job. They will not say so — they will simply not use it, and describe it as unreliable. This is quiet, common, and fatal, and spotting it is a human skill, not a dashboard.
  • Re-buy-in when people change. A new hire on the client’s team was not there for the training and has no attachment to any of it. Every staffing change is a small re-adoption problem.

The reframe worth internalising: you are not maintaining a system, you are maintaining a habit inside somebody else’s company. Systems stay where you put them. Habits decay unless something keeps reinforcing them, and this hire is that something.

The signals they watch

Concrete, not vibes. If they cannot tell you these per client from memory, they are not doing the job:

  • Is the workflow running, and at what volume compared to month one?
  • Who touched it last, and when?
  • Is the trained operator still in the seat?
  • How long since the client initiated contact, rather than responding to us?
  • Has anything in their business changed that the build assumed — new CRM, new team, new offer?

Build the ability to see this into the product. If checking usage means asking the client, you will not do it consistently and you will be relying on the client to self-report their own disengagement. That is not a metric, it is a hope. This is a real argument for instrumenting what you build from the first client onward, even crudely.

How technical do they need to be?

Technical enough to be credible about the thing you built, and not one step further. The line is clean: they must be able to read and explain. They do not need to be able to build.

The industry title for this is Technical Account Manager — sometimes Customer Success Engineer or post-sales Solutions Consultant. It is a real, established role, which matters: it means these people exist, they have a career track, and you can search for them by name rather than hoping to find a unicorn.

Six things they must be able to do unaided:

  • Open what was built and understand what it does without asking the delivery team. If every client question routes through delivery, you have not offloaded anything — you have added a relay.
  • Tell whether it is being used correctly, not merely whether it is being used. A client running a workflow the wrong way looks identical to a healthy account in any usage count, and produces none of the value.
  • Hold a real conversation with the client’s operator about how they are working, and catch the moment where they describe a workaround they invented because they misunderstood the system.
  • Triage correctly — is this a bug, a training gap, or an architecture problem? Bug goes to maintenance, training is theirs to fix, architecture goes to delivery. Someone who cannot tell these apart routes everything to delivery and burns your actual constraint on questions that were never technical.
  • Spot the adjacent build. Upsell here is not asking “anything else we can help with?” — it is hearing a client describe a manual process and recognising it as something your toolkit already solves. That requires knowing what you can build, not just what you did build for them.
  • Translate a technical outcome into the founder’s number. “The workflow ran 400 times” means nothing. “That is roughly 60 hours your team did not spend, and eleven leads that would have gone cold” is the renewal conversation.

Three things they do not need: to build anything, to debug code, or to design architecture. Hiring for those is how you end up paying a developer salary for someone who will get bored of the actual job within six months.

Why a genuinely non-technical person fails here, specifically. Not because they cannot learn it eventually — because credibility is required on day one. The moment the client’s technical person asks a real question and gets a deflection, the account manager becomes a switchboard. From that point on the client goes straight to your delivery team for everything, the relationship layer you paid for evaporates, and your constraint gets consumed by conversations that were never engineering.

Where these people come from

  • Ex-implementation consultants who preferred the relationship half of the job. The single best source — they already know how builds work and why they fail, and many find the account side more interesting than the build side.
  • Solutions engineers moving from pre-sales to post-sales. Technically fluent, already skilled at explaining systems to non-technical buyers.
  • Senior technical support moving up. Deep product knowledge, though the proactive habit has to be built — they are coming out of a queue.
  • Internally, from your own delivery team. The person who is good with clients but middling at builds is often an outstanding TAM, and they already know your stack, your builds and your failure patterns. Cheapest and lowest-risk path, and worth watching for from your first delivery hires onward.

The plateau trap

This model terminates by flattening — the last six months of the arc are identical to each other. An acquirer buys the next five years, not the last one.

The same business, same year, same margin, is worth 5–7× if the line is flat and 10–14× if it is still climbing. On $7m of EBITDA that is the difference between roughly $42m and $84m.

Which reframes the second acquisition channel entirely. It is not a marketing project — it is the thing that keeps the line rising through the window in which you would sell. It looks like a growth decision and it is a valuation decision worth tens of millions.

The right comparable is not SaaS and not an agency — it is an MSP

Managed Service Providers are outsourced IT departments for small and mid-sized companies. Helpdesk, network, servers, backups, security, procurement — flat monthly fee, hosted, embedded in the client’s operations. Structurally almost identical to what you are building, and there are two decades of valuation data on them.

The transformation that made them valuable is the one you are making. Before roughly 2005 the model was break-fix: something breaks, the client calls, you bill hourly. Unpredictable revenue and a perverse incentive — you earned more when their systems failed. The industry shifted to a flat monthly fee where the provider owns the outcome, and suddenly the incentive was for nothing to break.

Note what that transition actually was: not a technology change, a pricing and ownership change. Same work, different contract, completely different business — and the multiple went from 4–5× to 8–14× over the following decade as private equity worked out what it was looking at.

Their economics, for calibration: $1–10m revenue, 30–60 clients, 10–50 staff, $100–200 per seat per month. Gross margins 40–60%, EBITDA 10–20% (20–30% for the well-run), revenue per employee $150–200k.

You are structurally better than that on every line — around $600k revenue per head, because their margins are dragged down by helpdesk labour and low-margin hardware pass-through. The comparison is not aspirational. You are a higher-margin version of a shape that has already been proven and repeatedly bought.

Where you are structurally better than an MSP

Six real advantages, and the first two are the ones that matter most:

  • 1. You are not geographically bound. MSPs traditionally needed local presence — someone has to physically touch a server. That capped their addressable market to a metro area and is a large part of why the industry stayed fragmented and sub-scale. Nothing you build requires anyone to be anywhere. Your TAM is the language, not the postcode.
  • 2. Your acquisition engine is your own. MSPs grow by referral, local networking and trade association relationships — notoriously hard to scale, which is precisely why private equity rolled them up rather than growing them organically. Buying MSPs was easier than selling MSP services. You already own a repeatable acquisition machine, which is the exact capability the entire MSP industry lacked.
  • 3. Acquisition cost is liquidated at the point of sale. MSPs carry real sales cost against long cycles. The $897 audit means you are cash-positive on acquisition immediately.
  • 4. Delivery gets cheaper over time; theirs gets more expensive. MSP costs are technician wages, which rise every year. Your cost per client falls as builds standardise. Fundamentally opposite margin trajectories over a decade.
  • 5. No hardware pass-through. A large slice of MSP revenue is low-margin equipment resale that drags blended margin down. You have none of it — which is most of the gap between their $150–200k revenue per head and your ~$600k.
  • 6. No vendor margin squeeze. MSPs resell other people’s software and get squeezed when those vendors raise prices or go direct. The primitives you build on are getting cheaper, and you keep the difference.

Where they are better than you — worth knowing honestly

  • Defensive spend. IT gets paid in a recession because the alternative is not functioning. Growth infrastructure is discretionary. They are recession-resistant and you are not.
  • Higher retention. 90%+ annually, because nobody casually switches the company that holds their passwords and their compliance.
  • An established buyer pool. Twenty years of transactions, buyers who understand the model instantly, and comps on every deal. You will spend part of your sale process explaining what you are.
  • Regulatory lock-in. Compliance obligations bolt the client to the provider in a way an automation workflow does not.

The strategic read: you have the MSP’s economics without its two structural handicaps — geography and distribution. Those two are exactly what kept that industry fragmented and made it a rollup target rather than a category of large independent companies. You start with the thing they had to be acquired to obtain.

What you lack is their defensiveness. Which is the argument, again, for building menu items that the business cannot run without rather than ones that merely help it grow.

What separated a 12× MSP from a 5× MSP

This is a diligence checklist, and every item is buildable from client #1 at almost no cost:

  • Recurring as a share of revenue. Above 80% earned the premium; heavy project revenue was discounted hard. Your upfront fee is project revenue — the ratio is a valuation lever, not just a cashflow one.
  • Contract length. Multi-year beat month-to-month decisively.
  • Customer concentration. No client above roughly 10% of revenue.
  • Stack discipline. Providers who supported anything were destroyed on margin. Those who said “we support this stack only” had far better economics. Your menu discipline, proven empirically across twenty years and thousands of companies.
  • Documented process. Runbooks and SOPs are paid for directly at diligence.
  • Owner involvement. The provider whose founder still handled escalations took a discount. Every time, without exception.

Their failure modes are yours

The overlap is not coincidence — it is the same business shape producing the same failures:

  • Scope creep inside the contract. All-you-can-eat agreements where the client keeps asking and the provider cannot say no. Margin death by increments.
  • Under-pricing to win, then being locked to unprofitable clients permanently.
  • The owner is the best technician and never builds the bench.
  • Every client on a different stack, so no leverage ever compounds.
  • One catastrophic incident — an outage, a breach — and the account is gone.

Four things worth stealing

1. Per-unit pricing instead of a flat monthly fee

MSPs charge per seat or per device, so their revenue grows automatically as the client grows. A flat fee does not. If a client doubles their sales team, your fee arguably should move — that is expansion revenue with no sales conversation, and it converts your client’s growth into yours. It is also a far better retention story than hosting alone: you are priced like a partner rather than a vendor.

2. Tiered service levels

Bronze / silver / gold gives an upsell path that lives entirely inside the menu. No bespoke work required to grow an account.

3. The vCIO function

Good MSPs run a “virtual CIO” doing quarterly strategic planning with the client. That is the Client Success / TAM seat, and their version is worth copying in detail rather than reinventing.

4. Monthly value reporting

MSPs learned the hard way that doing the job perfectly makes you invisible, and invisible is a churn risk. If nothing breaks and nobody reports on what was prevented, the client starts wondering what they pay for.

Qualitative — the things that must be TRUE

These are not measured, they are verified. Each one is worth a chunk of multiple on its own.

  • The founder is not in delivery. Not “mostly not” — not at all, and demonstrably so for at least twelve months before a sale.
  • The founder is not the top salesperson. If you personally close most deals, the buyer is purchasing your calendar, and they know you will leave.
  • Documented process. Runbooks, SOPs, an onboarding playbook someone new can follow. This is the single cheapest multiple protection available and it costs you nothing but the habit.
  • A management team that stays. Buyers pay for the org, not the founder. A Head of Delivery who signs a retention package is worth more than a year of margin improvement.
  • Clean financials. Accrual accounting, reviewed or audited statements, revenue tied to signed contracts. Cash-basis books with no contracts behind them cannot be verified and get discounted on principle.
  • Assignable contracts. Change-of-control clauses that permit transfer. A book of business that legally cannot be sold is not a book of business.
  • Clear IP ownership. Everything built by contractors assigned in writing. This is a common and expensive surprise.
  • Security posture. For hosted systems touching client data: SOC 2, DPAs, incident response. Cheap to build early, brutal to retrofit under an LOI.
  • No single platform dependency that could be cut off. If one vendor’s API terminating would break half your book, that is a disclosed risk and it prices in.

Multiple killers — the things that must NOT happen

Any one of these can cost several turns of multiple, and some end a deal outright.

One client above 20% of revenue

Concentration risk is the most common reason a good business gets a mediocre price. The buyer models what happens if that client leaves, and prices the business as if they already have.

Month-to-month contracts

Revenue that can walk with thirty days’ notice is barely recurring revenue. This is a paperwork decision worth turns of multiple, and it is free to fix at signing and impossible to fix retroactively.

Flat or declining growth at the point of sale

The model plateaus by design. Selling into that plateau converts a 10–14× business into a 5–7× one with no other change.

A churn spike in the trailing twelve months

Buyers weight recent cohorts heavily. One bad year immediately before a sale is worth more to them than three good ones before it.

Key delivery people with no contracts

Someone who holds the knowledge, has no non-solicit, and could leave with clients is a diligence finding that either kills the deal or funds an escrow.

Unprofitable legacy clients you never repriced

The grandfathered-pricing problem, surfacing years later as a margin drag the buyer will model forward forever.

Undisclosed scope creep

Clients receiving work outside the contract because somebody once said yes. It shows up in diligence as unbilled cost of delivery and calls the whole margin story into question.

A material incident with no runbook

An outage or breach is survivable. An outage or breach with no documented response, and no evidence you fixed the cause, is a governance failure — and buyers price governance failures as character rather than as bad luck.

The pattern worth noticing: almost nothing on either list is expensive, and almost nothing can be fixed quickly. Contract terms, documentation, concentration limits, security posture and founder distance are all cheap decisions made early and impossible to retrofit late. That is the entire argument for reading this page now rather than in year four.

Where the analogy breaks — and it matters

IT is defensive spend. You are offensive spend.

An MSP sells something every business needs and nobody cancels — when times get hard, IT still gets paid because the alternative is not functioning. You sell growth infrastructure, which is discretionary. Your revenue is more cyclical than theirs, and in a downturn you will churn harder.

That argues for making the builds feel operational rather than promotional. The closer your workflows sit to “the business does not run without this”, the more you inherit the MSP’s defensive characteristic instead of an agency’s exposure. It is a design principle for what goes on the menu, not just a marketing frame.

The honest summary

Plan for $20–40m. Treat $100m+ as the upside case. It requires all six of: retention landing at 12+ months, delegation genuinely working, menu discipline holding, a second channel existing, low founder dependency at diligence, and still growing at the point of sale.

None of those are technology problems. Every one is a discipline problem, which is the good news — they are all decisions rather than discoveries, and most of them are free to make correctly starting now.

Pricing — a working brainstorm

Nothing here is settled. The current numbers are placeholders that will not survive contact with the first ten sales conversations, and that is fine — what matters is getting the architecture right before the first contract, because two of these decisions are permanent.

Decided — the locked architecture

These are settled. Everything below is the reasoning; open questions are at the bottom.

THE INITIAL SALE IS PRESCRIBED. THERE IS NO À LA CARTE.

The client is sold the whole stack — Pipeline Engine, Wingman, SalesPulse, Revenue Dashboard — at one price. They do not see a line-item menu and they do not choose components.

Why: the moment “Automated SDR — $7k/mo” appears on a page, the buyer puts it next to a $97/month tool and you have lost. Every individual component is commoditised; the assembled system is not, because nobody else sells it assembled. Line items invite exactly the comparison that kills the deal, and a menu invites them to talk themselves out of the pieces that make the whole thing work.

1. One prescribed package at the initial sale. No component selection, no line items shown.

2. Line-item pricing exists internally only. Surfaced to a client in exactly one situation: an upsell, for the single item being added. Never as a menu.

3. Launch pricing: $20k upfront + $3–4k/month. Deliberately below target. Price elasticity is unknown at zero clients, and erring toward letting more people in buys learning that no amount of modelling will.

4. Target pricing once proven: ~$22k + $7.5k/month. That monthly is not arbitrary — it is the level that holds recurring share above 75%, which is the difference between the 7–10× and 10–14× band.

5. A founding cohort, contractually repriced — with the future number written in. Capped at ten clients or six months, whichever comes first. The contract states the specific price at first renewal, not merely that pricing may change.

6. The recurring is never discounted. Not at launch, not on upsell, not on the tenth item.

7. Discounts come out of the upfront only, hard-capped in advance.

8. Value sets the price. Complexity sets the floor. Complexity decides whether something goes on the menu at all, not what it costs.

9. An annual uplift clause from day one, capped, exercised every year at a small number rather than saved up.

10. Cohort pricing. New clients pay current list; existing clients are grandfathered at signing.

The monthly is the protected number. Bundling was never what underpriced this — the low recurring was. Old shape $20k + $3k; target shape $22k + $7.5k. The upfront barely moves; the monthly is 2.5×. Negotiate the upfront freely. Every $1k conceded on the recurring costs roughly $100k of exit value at these multiples. The upfront is cash; the recurring is the asset.

The failure mode of the founding cohort is not the low price. It is never raising it. By month seven those ten clients are your friends and your case studies, and a vague “pricing may change” clause is one nobody has the stomach to exercise. Writing the future number into the original contract converts an uncomfortable negotiation into a reminder of something already agreed. That single drafting choice is worth more than the clause itself.

And cap the cohort by count, not only by time. A six-month window fails precisely when things go well — twenty-five clients signed cheap in a good six months is twenty-five permanently sub-scale accounts consuming delivery slots at maturity.

Negotiation levers, in order

Removing a system to cut the price is the last lever, not the first — it means a worse result, a weaker case study, and less reason for them to stay.

1. Extend payment terms on the upfront — same total, easier cheque.
2. Discount the upfront against a longer commitment (12 or 24 months).
3. Only then, remove a system — cutting both its upfront and its monthly, never just the upfront.

The upsell architecture — à la carte, after they are a client

Every menu item carries its own upfront and its own recurring, both set by value with complexity as the floor. Indicative shape: $5–10k upfront and $2–5k/month per item, depending on the item.

This applies to expansion, never to the first sale. Once someone is a client, adding a system is a new line — its own upfront plus its own monthly, stacked onto what they already have. That is the only moment a client ever sees an individual item price.

A minimum on total spend is better than a minimum on item count. It self-adjusts — two expensive items clears it, four cheap ones clears it, and you never have to arbitrate. The client experiences it as “pick enough that this is worth building properly” rather than as an arbitrary rule.

Someone who wants only item one at $5k is told, honestly: we can do it, but it is not worth allocating a delivery team to. Pick more, or we are not the right fit. That is a qualification filter and a valuation decision at the same time — recurring share rises with item count, and recurring share is what sets the multiple band.

Expansion is frictionless. An existing client adding an item five months in is a new line, not a renegotiation — a new upfront plus a new monthly, stacked onto what they already have.

What can still go wrong

Cherry-picking the cheap end

With items spanning $5–10k, clients rationally choose the cheapest combination that clears the minimum. Over time the book skews toward your lowest-value items — which are usually also the least sticky and the easiest to cancel. Worth considering a rule that at least one anchor item is included, or pricing the cheap items so they are unattractive standalone. Currently unaddressed.

Discounts drifting past the cost saving

Handled by policy — hard caps, set in advance, and stacking discounts kept modest. The thing that makes the caps correct rather than arbitrary is measuring real setup hours for item one versus item four on the first clients. Until that number exists the caps are a sensible guess.

Quote complexity as the menu grows

Five items with two prices each, plus capped discounts and a minimum, is explainable on a call. Fifteen items is a configurator, and it slows every conversation. Cap the menu at a size one person can hold in their head.

The capacity model no longer matches the pricing model

Mix-and-match means delivery hours are per item, while the capacity model still uses a single number for a whole onboarding. Not wrong yet — it is an average — but it will need revisiting once real per-item hours exist, because a client taking six items and one taking two are not the same delivery job.

The two axes — with one correction

You named the right two inputs: complexity to build, and perceived value to the client. But they should not be used the same way.

Complexity sets the floor. Value sets the price.

Pricing on complexity is cost-plus — it is an agency billing hours in disguise, and it produces a perverse result: your most expensive items become the ones you are worst at building. As you get better and a build drops from 85 hours to 34, cost-plus pricing says charge less for the same outcome. That is backwards.

Complexity belongs in the build / do-not-build decision. If the value a client will pay for sits below what it costs you to deliver, the item does not go on the menu. Above that line, complexity is irrelevant to the price.

How to actually price an item

A method rather than a guess, for each item on the day-one menu:

1. Estimate the annual value to the client. Hours saved × loaded rate, or revenue gained, or cost avoided. Be specific and conservative — you will have to defend this number in the audit.
2. Price the annual contract at 10–20% of that value. The standard B2B heuristic. Below 10% you are leaving money on the table; above 25% the buyer starts doing the maths against alternatives.
3. Split it between upfront and monthly using your chosen ratio.
4. Sanity-check against your floor. If the resulting price does not clear delivery cost with real margin, the item does not belong on the menu.

Worked shape: a workflow saving 20 hours a month at $50/hr loaded = $12k of annual value → price $1.2–2.4k per year. A workflow producing $200k of incremental pipeline is a completely different number. Items tied to revenue price far higher than items tied to time saved, which is an argument for weighting the menu toward revenue-generating automations rather than efficiency ones.

The launch move

You cannot price correctly before selling anything, but you can avoid the one unfixable error: anchoring too low. Price is grandfathered at signing, so an early low price is permanent.

Set list price where you think it should be in eighteen months, then discount the first cohort explicitly as founding clients. The anchor stays high, the discount is a gift with a stated reason and an end date, and cohort two pays list without anyone feeling that prices went up. A discount you can withdraw is infinitely easier than a price you have to raise.

Open questions

  • What is the day-one menu? Every price question is downstream of this and it does not exist yet. Nothing else here can be settled first.
  • Does recurring start at signing or at go-live? Currently go-live, which gifts the client roughly three months of fees. Most SaaS bills from signature. Worth $9–21k of lifetime value per client, and it is a decision, not a law.
  • Is an anchor item required? Open. Without one, the cheapest-combination-that-clears-$20k is the rational client choice, and the book drifts toward low-value items.
  • One onboarding fee plus smaller per-item upfronts, or the per-item upfronts alone? The shared setup cost is real and sits in item one either way — the question is whether to name it separately.
  • Does the $897 audit anchor badly? A jump from $897 to $20k+ is roughly 22×. Probably fine — the audit should feel trivially cheap relative to the build — but worth watching in the first ten calls.
