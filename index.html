Questions we'll have to answer to scale

Things the model surfaced but deliberately doesn't answer. Each is parked here with what we already know and what would settle it — so we're not rediscovering the same tension in three months.

01open

Should onboarding and long-term account maintenance eventually be separate people?

You can only run so many onboardings a month, but recurring accounts stack indefinitely. Every client added keeps drawing maintenance hours forever, so the maintenance load grows while onboarding capacity stays flat. At some point one person cannot hold both.

The tension: the dev who built a client's system maintains it far more cheaply than anyone else, because the context is in their head. Handing it over means paying someone to slowly learn what Geri already knows. But context in a head is a choice, not a law. The same factory work that takes onboarding from 100 hours to 40 — specs, reusable adapters, runbooks, monitoring — is exactly what makes maintenance transferable. One investment, not two.

What we already know
  • At 96 of Geri's delivery hours a month and 12 hrs upkeep per client, maintenance alone consumes him entirely at 8 live clients.
  • The model puts the two-person ceiling at 9–14 live clients — so the maintenance wall and the capacity ceiling arrive at the same moment. Not a distant problem.
  • Halving upkeep to 6 hrs moves the wall to 16 clients — the same lever that fixes everything else in this model.
  • A likely answer is splitting by problem type, not by person: recurring failures, monitoring and data hygiene delegate cleanly; novel breakage and change requests don't.
What would settle it

Track, from client #1, what share of maintenance is recurring versus novel. If most fixes are the same handful of failure modes across clients, maintenance is delegable and the hire is cheap. If every client breaks differently, it stays welded to the builder — and that argues for tighter qualification, not a bigger team.

02verify

How many hours does a live client actually consume each month?

The most load-bearing assumption in the model, and currently a guess. It sets the capacity ceiling, the parity price for the recurring tier, and when the maintenance wall arrives.

What we already know
  • Currently assumed 12 hrs/month per live client, scaling down with efficiency.
  • At 12 hrs, recurring parity sits at $2,400/mo — so $3,000 is only 1.25× ahead.
  • At 6 hrs, parity drops to $1,200 and $3,000 becomes 2.5× ahead. Halving upkeep does the same work as doubling the price, without a pricing conversation.
What would settle it

Geri logs maintenance hours per client for the first two clients. Two months of real data replaces the most consequential guess in the model.

03verify

What does it actually take to make the apps usable by outside companies — and when do we pay for it?

The machines were built single-tenant, running on our own data. Client #1 forces the question: separate logins per client, isolation so one client can never see another's pipeline, per-client secrets, and config that isn't hardcoded to QS. In plain terms, it's the difference between a house you built for yourself and an apartment building — same structure, but it now needs its own front doors and walls that actually work.

It was briefly in the model at 80 hours, then taken out, and taking it out was right: it is paid once and never again, so it isn't a per-client cost and doesn't belong among levers that are all variable. But it doesn't disappear — it's a prerequisite that lands before any revenue does.

What we already know
  • The 80 hr figure is a guess, and the least-informed number in anything we've estimated.
  • If the apps were written with tenancy in mind it could be 30 hrs. If QS is hardcoded through the schema it could be 150.
  • At 160 delivery hours a month, 80 hrs is half of your first month spent before client #1 produces anything.
What would settle it

Geri reviews the current apps against one question: what breaks if a second company's data lands in this? An afternoon of his time replaces the guess, and it's the one estimate that changes when you can start.

04open

Should efficiency improve with clients delivered rather than with time?

The model currently improves efficiency on a monthly timer. But you learn by shipping, not by waiting — a month with two onboardings should teach more than a month with none. Driving the curve off completed clients would make early throughput self-reinforcing, and would correctly punish a slow start.

What would settle it

Straightforward to change once there's real data on how much cheaper client #3 was than client #1.

05open

Do we refuse clients on non-standard stacks?

A client on an exotic CRM could cost 2–3× a normal onboarding and eat a month. Worse, their maintenance never standardises — so they stay welded to Geri permanently rather than ever becoming delegable. A stronger reason to decline than the onboarding hours alone.

What would settle it

Probably a qualification rule in the audit — we deploy onto these systems — rather than a lever in the model.

06parked

The model is deliberately pessimistic about retention. How much?

Expansion revenue is switched off, referrals aren't modelled, and a retained client's biggest hidden benefit — that it needs no sales or pre-sales time at all — is invisible here. Every one of those pushes the same way, so any conclusion this model reaches about keeping clients is a floor, not a forecast.

07parked

The hiring extension — what happens past the two-person ceiling?

Phase 2 of the model. Delivery hires first, then when a commission-only closer becomes viable — they need 5 deals a month to clear $10k at 10% of upfront, well beyond what two people deliver.
